\section{Experiments}\label{sec:exp}

\stitle{Setup.}
All code is C++23, single-threaded, compiled at \texttt{-O3}.
Three machines: a laptop (Apple M-series) and two Ubuntu 22.04 servers (GCC 11.4, 96 cores, 503~GB); no timing run shared a machine with another timing run of ours, and the servers carried a background load of 1 to 8 from other users.
Datasets: \numgraphs{} graphs from five families (collaboration, citation, web, product, social and communication), from 1,005 to 4,049,537 vertices, with largest cliques from 7 to 500 vertices; Table~\ref{tab:size} lists them.
The baseline \strees is one tree and one DFS array per size with all core values stored (Section~\ref{sec:prelim}); its size is computed from the same decomposition, and its query latency is measured by building it in the same process as the index from the same decomposition and running the same queries on both.
Queries are drawn from the active vertices and their sizes $2\le s\le\omg{v}$: 20,000 community queries at the vertex's own level ($k=\core{s}{v}$), 20,000 at half that level, 1,000 at $k=1$, and 200,000 value queries; every latency is the median of five passes after one warm-up.
Every number in the tables is generated from the recorded JSON evidence.

\expsection{Case study: the right clique size}
The index exists because the size is part of the query; the first two experiments ask, on graphs with real semantics, what the size buys.
Three SNAP graphs come with ground-truth communities (the top 5,000 by quality): \dblp (publication venues), com-amazon (product categories) and com-youtube (user groups).
For every member $v$ of every ground-truth community $C$, every size $s\le\omg{v}$ and every level $k$, the $(s,k)$-nucleus of $v$ is scored by its F1 against $C$; the whole $(s,k)$ space of a query is scanned from the index without listing a single community, since $|N\cap C|$ is counted through the members of $C$ (own node inside the node's subtree) and $|N|$ comes from the run bounds.
Table~\ref{tab:casegt} compares the $k$-core index (size 2, best level), the triangle size ($s=3$, best level), the best single size for the graph, and the best size or the best (size, level) chosen per query, and Table~\ref{tab:casehist} gives where the best size falls.
On \dblp no single size is right: the best size spreads over 2 to 8, choosing it per query gives 3.6$\times$ the F1 of the $k$-core index and 1.2$\times$ the best fixed size, and for 74 percent of the queries some size other than 2 beats every $k$-core.
On com-amazon the triangle size alone is nearly as good as the per-query choice (0.824 against 0.851) and both are 1.8$\times$ the $k$-core index.
On com-youtube every cohesive model is far from the ground-truth groups (F1 below 0.04, a known property of that dataset) and the comparison says nothing.
The per-query choice costs 1 to 50~$\mu$s from the index; without it, each size would be one decomposition of the graph.

\expsection{Case study: the size as a zoom on Amazon}
com-amazon joined with the SNAP product metadata (titles, groups, category paths; 334,852 of the 334,863 products) lets the communities be read.
Table~\ref{tab:caseamazon} follows 5,000 products drawn uniformly among those active at size 3 through their own-level community at every size, scored by purity: the share of members that share a leaf category with the query product, and the share that share a subject (the third level of the category tree, such as Jazz, Computers \& Internet or Science Fiction \& Fantasy).
At size 2, the $k$-core, three quarters of the products sit in one core of 157,331 products at 10 percent purity; size 3 brings the median community to 38 products at 44 percent, size 4 to 11 products at 70 percent, size 5 to 8 products at 79 percent, and from size 4 on the community's dominant subject covers 99 percent of its members.
Table~\ref{tab:caseexamples} lists six products.
``Kind of Blue'' goes from 36,878 products (1 percent jazz) at size 2 to 23 Blue Note albums at size 3 and to six albums at size 5, the canonical jazz shelf; ``Introduction to Algorithms'' from the 157,331-product core to 57 computing books at size 3 and 13 theory textbooks at size 4; the Godfather soundtrack to 28 products at size 5 whose category purity is only 0.32 but which are one shelf, the four soundtracks with Jerry Vale, Lou Monte and ``Italian American Classics'', a community the category tree has no name for.
Every community after size 2 is a handful of ranges and is listed in under 50~ns.

\expsection{Where the bytes go}
On the collaboration, web and product graphs the per-size trees with their run arrays are 58 to 74 percent of the index and the chain records 23 to 41 percent; on the social and citation graphs, where chains are shorter, the chain records reach 44 to 58 percent, most of it the trajectories (one node id per chain per size).
The map is 3 to 13 percent.
Core values are a small part everywhere once residues and per-size widths are in place.
The certified tail (Theorem~\ref{thm:tail}) is what makes them small: the share of active (vertex, size) pairs whose value must be stored ranges from \storedmin{} percent (\webuk, where one residue suffices for 23.5 million pairs) to \storedmax{} percent (\emailcore), median \storedmedian{} percent (Table~\ref{tab:size}); it is lowest on the graphs with large cliques, where most of a vertex's values lie in its tail, and highest on the sparse social and citation graphs, where $\sigma$ is close to $\omega$.
Stored residues are shared by the vertices of a chain, so the stored values are fewer still: \dblp stores 9,737 residues for 1,246,933 active pairs.

\expsection{Design study}
Table~\ref{tab:layouts} isolates the steps on the five graphs of the design study.
Trees over twin classes do not help (twin classes have 1.03 to 1.28 vertices); trees over chains with an explicit vertex-to-chain map are 2.5$\times$ to 4.5$\times$ smaller than \strees; aligned labels remove the map and turn answers into ranges; run arrays with entry points and per-size widths give the final sizes and make the location constant-time.
The skyline deduplication of SGL \cite{SGL}, which we also implemented over chains, was 0.99$\times$ to 2.28$\times$ smaller than the corresponding tree layout but 2 to 3 times slower to query and is dominated by chains; it is not part of the index.

\expsection{Query latency}
Table~\ref{tab:queries} reports, at the own level, the time to locate a community (climb and two entry reads), the number of ranges of the answer, its size, the time to list it as vertex labels, and the value query; beside the two community times stand those of \strees, measured in the same process on the same queries (own-node lookup, parent-pointer climb, one memory copy of the DFS slice).
Locating takes \locmin{} to \locmax{}~ns (median \locmedian{}~ns); a community of 1.9 million vertices on the 4-million-vertex coauthorship graph is located in 11~ns and listed in 167~$\mu$s.
At the own level \strees locates in about \stlocown{} of that time: two dependent loads against the map, trajectory and entry reads of the index.
Listing costs \pervmin{} to \pervmax{}~ns per vertex on the graphs whose mean answer has at least a thousand vertices, and on the two graphs with answers below a hundred vertices the fixed cost of a query dominates.
Against the memory copy of \strees the listing time ranges from \stmin$\times$ to \stmax$\times$ (median \stmedian, faster on \stfaster{} of \stpoints{} (graph, level) points): a run costs eight bytes to read however many labels it expands to, so the index is faster where the answer is a few long ranges (the social, product and web graphs) and slower where it is fragmented (\emailcore, \textsf{cit-HepPh}, \textsf{ca-AstroPh}, 2 to 5 vertices per range), where one range costs as much as tens of copied labels.
Value queries take \valmin{} to \valmax{}~ns (median \valmedian{}~ns); the slowest is \webuk, whose binomial table for cliques of 500 vertices no longer fits the first-level cache.

Figure~\ref{fig:regimes} shows the three levels of the workload for both structures.
Listing time is linear in the size of the answer at every level, between 0.05 and 0.5~ns per vertex above a fixed cost of a few nanoseconds, for the index and for the copy alike.
Locating costs a median of \ownmedian{}~ns at the own level, \halfmedian{}~ns at half the level (the climb from the own node to an ancestor) and \rootmedian{}~ns at $k=1$: the jump pointers make the climb to the root as cheap as no climb at all, and the half-level climb, which stops at an interior node, costs at most \halfmax{}~ns.
The parent-pointer climb of \strees has no such bound: at the two deeper levels it costs up to \stlocdeepmax$\times$ the index's time (1,086~ns against 109~ns at the half level of the coauthorship graph, whose hierarchy has 5,543 levels), which is why the index keeps the derived jump pointers although they are a third of its per-node bytes.

\expsection{Latency by clique size and by answer size}
Figure~\ref{fig:profile} separates the two variables that the mixed workload averages, on the four largest graphs of the second server, for the index and for \strees.
Panel (a) draws 5,000 own-level queries per clique size, the vertex uniform among the vertices active at that size: the index locates in 8 to 44~ns at every size, highest at $s=4$ to $6$, where the per-size trees have the most nodes, and \strees in 2 to 11~ns, because its own-node lookup is two dependent loads.
Panel (b) lists those answers: at $s=2$ and $3$, where the answers are large and consist of a few long runs, the index lists 1.0$\times$ to 3.2$\times$ faster than the memory copy; from $s=4$ on, where the own-level answers shrink to hundreds or a few thousand vertices spread over many ranges, the copy is 2$\times$ to 5$\times$ faster.
Panel (c) sorts the 20,000 own-level queries of the fixed workload by answer size into deciles: below $10^5$ vertices the copy is 2$\times$ to 3$\times$ faster, and above it the two coincide within 10 percent.
The index trades a constant per range for a constant per label, so it lists at the speed of the copy where answers are big and contiguous and behind it where they are small and fragmented; the bytes it saves (Table~\ref{tab:size}) do not depend on the answer.

\expsection{Scalability}
Figure~\ref{fig:scale} follows two graphs through vertex-induced random samples of 20, 40, 60 and 80 percent of their vertices (every vertex kept independently, seed fixed) up to the full graph; a sample keeps about the square of the fraction of the edges, so the curves show a graph family growing in size and in density at once, with the largest cliques of \textsf{web-BerkStan} going from 45 to 202 vertices.
The index grows about linearly with the vertices (2.9 to 3.2 bytes per vertex on \textsf{cit-Patents}, 5.3 to 12.6 on \textsf{web-BerkStan} as its cliques grow), \strees grows with the vertices times the sizes they take part in, and the ratio between the two rises along each curve, from 7.7$\times$ to 12.1$\times$ and from 6.4$\times$ to 19.2$\times$.
The build, which is the all-size peel, grows faster than the graph (0.56 to 19.5 seconds over five times the vertices of \textsf{cit-Patents}, 0.21 to 65.6 seconds on \textsf{web-BerkStan}), and \cnd run once per size grows faster still, its ratio to the index build rising from 6.6$\times$ to 18$\times$ along the \textsf{cit-Patents} curve and lying between 61$\times$ and 135$\times$ along the \textsf{web-BerkStan} one; the peak memory is the clique tree of the peel.
Query latency does not move: locating stays between 21 and 28~ns and listing between 0.09 and 0.21~ns per vertex at every size, against \strees on the same samples.

\expsection{Against the single-size decomposition}
The published counting-based nucleus decomposition \cnd \cite{Zhang2026NucleusCounting} computes the $(1,s)$ core values of one size per run: it builds a clique path index and peels while rewriting the index.
Obtaining every size with it means one run per size; Table~\ref{tab:prior} sums those runs over $s=2,\dots,s_{\max}$ on \priorgraphs{} (graph, machine) pairs, both as whole-process wall time and as the in-process build and peel timers alone, against the whole chain index build (clique tree, all-size peel, trees, chains, run arrays) on the same machine.
(A faster single-size peel that keeps the index fixed exists; it lowers the constant of each run and leaves the number of runs, one per size, unchanged.)
Counting only \cnd's clique-tree build and peel, the chain index is built \priormin$\times$ (com-amazon, six sizes) to \priormax$\times$ (\webuk, 499 sizes) faster, median \priormedian$\times$; counting \cnd's whole processes, which reload and sort the graph for every size, the factors are larger still.
The clique tree is built once instead of once per size, and the sizes share their peel through bounds and certificates; the ratio grows with the number of sizes: on web-BerkStan (201 sizes) the 2.3 hours of \cnd become 66 seconds, and on \webuk{} 15.7 hours become 70 seconds.
The same table is the cost of answering a community query without an index: the smallest possible online answer is one decomposition at the queried size, seconds to minutes per query on these graphs, against the nanoseconds of Table~\ref{tab:queries}.
\cnd stores no hierarchy, so the storage comparison stays with \strees.

\expsection{Correctness}
For every labelled graph on at most six vertices, 200 random graphs on 7 to 10 vertices, split graphs and $K_8$ (\selfgraphs{} graphs) plus $K_{300}$, the nuclei were computed by brute force from clique enumeration and union-find, and every $(v,s,k)$ with $1\le k\le\core{s}{v}$ was queried: \selfcommunities{} community queries compared as sets, \selfvalues{} value checks, and \selfmembers{} membership checks across the build form, the compact form and the form loaded from disk, with exact-integer and double values, under a release build and under AddressSanitizer and UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer.
On the real graphs the number of canonical nodes agrees with an independent implementation, and the index of a graph is byte-identical across the two servers even when the input files list the edges in different orders.
