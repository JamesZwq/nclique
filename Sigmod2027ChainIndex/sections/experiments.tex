\section{Experiments}\label{sec:exp}

\stitle{Setup.}
All code is C++23, single-threaded, compiled at \texttt{-O3}.
Three machines: a laptop (Apple M-series) and two Ubuntu 22.04 servers (GCC 11.4, 96 cores, 503~GB); no timing run shared a machine with another timing run of ours, and the servers carried a background load of 1 to 8 from other users.
Datasets: \numgraphs{} graphs from five families (collaboration, citation, web, product, social and communication), from 1,005 to 4,049,537 vertices, with largest cliques from 7 to 500 vertices; Table~\ref{tab:size} lists them.
The baseline \strees is one tree and one DFS array per size with all core values stored (Section~\ref{sec:prelim}); its size is computed from the same decomposition, and its query latency was measured with our own implementation on the five laptop graphs of the design study and on the seven graphs of the second server.
Queries are drawn from the active vertices and their sizes $2\le s\le\omg{v}$: 20,000 community queries at the vertex's own level ($k=\core{s}{v}$), 20,000 at half that level, 1,000 at $k=1$, and 200,000 value queries; every latency is the median of five passes after one warm-up.
Every number in the tables is generated from the recorded JSON evidence.

\expsection{Size against one tree per size}
Table~\ref{tab:size} gives, for every graph, the number of chains, the vertices per chain, the share of core values that are stored explicitly, the index size and the size of \strees.
The index is \ratiomin$\times$ to \ratiomax$\times$ smaller, median \ratiomedian$\times$.
The ratio follows the vertices per chain: \emailcore (1,005 vertices, 667 chains) is the worst case at \ratiomin$\times$, and \webuk (129,632 vertices, 839 chains, cliques up to 500) the best at \ratiomax$\times$; the two coauthorship graphs with the largest cliques compress by 45.8$\times$ and 12.0$\times$, the web graphs by 5.7$\times$ to 48.7$\times$, the social graphs by 2.9$\times$ to 15.9$\times$.
The label permutation, if the graph is not stored in the index's order, adds $4n$ bytes and lowers the ratios to 1.25$\times$--8.8$\times$ on the laptop set.

\expsection{Where the bytes go}
On the collaboration, web and product graphs the per-size trees with their run arrays are 58 to 74 percent of the index and the chain records 23 to 41 percent; on the social and citation graphs, where chains are shorter, the chain records reach 44 to 58 percent, most of it the trajectories (one node id per chain per size).
The map is 3 to 13 percent.
Core values are a small part everywhere once residues and per-size widths are in place.
The certified tail (Theorem~\ref{thm:tail}) is what makes them small: the share of active (vertex, size) pairs whose value must be stored ranges from \storedmin{} percent (\webuk, where one residue suffices for 23.5 million pairs) to \storedmax{} percent (\emailcore), median \storedmedian{} percent (Table~\ref{tab:size}); it is lowest on the graphs with large cliques, where most of a vertex's values lie in its tail, and highest on the sparse social and citation graphs, where $\sigma$ is close to $\omega$.
Stored residues are shared by the vertices of a chain, so the stored values are fewer still: \dblp stores 9,737 residues for 1,246,933 active pairs.

\expsection{Design study}
Table~\ref{tab:layouts} isolates the steps on the five graphs of the design study.
Trees over twin classes do not help (twin classes have 1.03 to 1.28 vertices); trees over chains with an explicit vertex-to-chain map are 2.5$\times$ to 4.5$\times$ smaller than \strees and already faster to list because a community is a few chain slices; aligned labels remove the map and turn answers into ranges; run arrays with entry points and per-size widths give the final sizes and make the location constant-time.
The skyline deduplication of SGL \cite{SGL}, which we also implemented over chains, was 0.99$\times$ to 2.28$\times$ smaller than the corresponding tree layout but 2 to 3 times slower to query and is dominated by chains; it is not part of the index.

\expsection{A compressor as the yardstick}
Table~\ref{tab:compress} applies \texttt{xz -9e}, the strongest general-purpose compressor available to us, to \strees and to the index file on the laptop graphs.
\strees is written with the labels the decomposition works with (the degeneracy order of the input) and, separately, with the index's aligned labels.
Under the input labels \texttt{xz} shrinks \strees by 10$\times$ to 23$\times$, to 0.18 to 0.48 of the index: most bytes of \strees are repetition across sizes, which a stream compressor with a 64~MB window finds and no random-access structure can use; under the aligned labels \strees compresses 1.0$\times$ to 1.8$\times$ further, the labels alone carrying the chain structure.
The index file itself compresses by a further 5.0$\times$ to 7.7$\times$: its per-size arrays (parents, subtree sizes, entry points, run bounds, trajectories) are plain 4-byte words, which is what makes a query a handful of aligned loads, and every narrower coding would put decoding into the query path.
The size comparison of Table~\ref{tab:size} is therefore between structures that answer queries in place; the compressor is the yardstick for what any representation could reach, and none of the compressed forms answers a query without decompressing (34~MB for the \strees of com-youtube).

\expsection{Query latency}
Table~\ref{tab:queries} reports, at the own level, the time to locate a community (climb and two entry reads), the number of ranges of the answer, the time to copy the range list, and the time to expand it into vertex labels, together with the listing time of \strees where it was measured and the value query.
Locating takes \locmin{} to \locmax{}~ns on every graph (median \locmedian{}~ns); a community of 1.9 million vertices on the 4-million-vertex coauthorship graph is located in 11~ns and listed in 167~$\mu$s.
Listing costs \pervmin{} to \pervmax{}~ns per vertex on the graphs whose mean answer has at least a thousand vertices; the per-vertex cost is lowest where runs are long and highest on the most fragmented graphs (\emailcore, 2.3 vertices per range), and on the two graphs with answers below a hundred vertices the fixed cost of a query dominates.
Against the memory copy of \strees, measured on \stgraphs{} graphs at the three levels, expansion is faster on \stfaster{} of the \stpoints{} (graph, level) points, by \stmin$\times$ to \stmax$\times$: a run costs eight bytes to read however many labels it expands to.
Value queries take \valmin{} to \valmax{}~ns (median \valmedian{}~ns); the slowest is \webuk, whose binomial table for cliques of 500 vertices no longer fits the first-level cache.

Figure~\ref{fig:regimes} shows the three levels of the workload.
Listing time is linear in the size of the answer at every level, between 0.05 and 0.5~ns per vertex above a fixed cost of a few nanoseconds, because the number of ranges grows with the answer while the cost per range stays constant.
Locating costs a median of \locmedian{}~ns at the own level, \halfmedian{}~ns at half the level (the climb from the own node to an ancestor) and \rootmedian{}~ns at $k=1$: the jump pointers make the climb to the root as cheap as no climb at all, and the half-level climb, which stops at an interior node, costs at most \halfmax{}~ns.

\expsection{Against the single-size decomposition}
The published counting-based nucleus decomposition \cnd \cite{Zhang2026NucleusCounting} computes the $(1,s)$ core values of one size per run: it builds a clique path index and peels while rewriting the index.
Obtaining every size with it means one run per size; Table~\ref{tab:prior} sums those runs over $s=2,\dots,s_{\max}$ on \priorgraphs{} (graph, machine) pairs, both as whole-process wall time and as the in-process build and peel timers alone, against the whole chain index build (clique tree, all-size peel, trees, chains, run arrays) on the same machine.
(A faster single-size peel that keeps the index fixed exists; it lowers the constant of each run and leaves the number of runs, one per size, unchanged.)
Counting only \cnd's clique-tree build and peel, the chain index is built \priormin$\times$ (com-amazon, six sizes) to \priormax$\times$ (ca-HepPh, 238 sizes) faster, median \priormedian$\times$; counting \cnd's whole processes, which reload and sort the graph for every size, the factors are larger still.
The clique tree is built once instead of once per size, and the sizes share their peel through bounds and certificates; the ratio grows with the number of sizes, and on web-BerkStan (201 sizes) the 2.3 hours of \cnd become 66 seconds.
The same table is the cost of answering a community query without an index: the smallest possible online answer is one decomposition at the queried size, seconds to minutes per query on these graphs, against the nanoseconds of Table~\ref{tab:queries}.
\cnd stores no hierarchy, so the storage comparison stays with \strees.

\expsection{Correctness}
For every labelled graph on at most six vertices, 200 random graphs on 7 to 10 vertices, split graphs and $K_8$ (\selfgraphs{} graphs) plus $K_{300}$, the nuclei were computed by brute force from clique enumeration and union-find, and every $(v,s,k)$ with $1\le k\le\core{s}{v}$ was queried: \selfcommunities{} community queries compared as sets, \selfvalues{} value checks, and \selfmembers{} membership checks across the build form, the compact form and the form loaded from disk, with exact-integer and double values, under a release build and under AddressSanitizer and UndefinedBehaviorSanitizer.
On the real graphs the number of canonical nodes agrees with an independent implementation, and the index of a graph is byte-identical across the two servers even when the input files list the edges in different orders.
