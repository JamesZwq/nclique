This section describes the hierarchy of one size, that is, the forest that the cores of that size form under inclusion, and the index that stores one such forest per size.
% 2026-09-22 filler pass: the preview of Sections 4 to 7 removed; the bridge is the last sentence of the section.

\stitle{The tree of one size.}
Fix a size $s$.
%
% 2026-09-22 compaction: the standard facts of the hierarchy (Sariyuce et al.) with one reason each; nine sentences for twenty.
Two cores of size $s$ are disjoint or nested: if an $\Nuc{s}{k}$ $K$ and an $\Nuc{s}{k'}$ $K'$ with $k\le k'$ share a vertex, their union has the two properties of Definition~\ref{def:nucleus} at level $k$, so it is $K$ by maximality, and $K'\subseteq K$.
%
One vertex set can be a core at several levels.
\begin{definition}[canonical node, own node]\label{def:node}
A \textit{canonical node} of size $s$ is a vertex set that is an $\Nuc{s}{k}$ for at least one level $k$.
%
Its \textit{top} is the largest such $k$.
%
The canonical nodes of size $s$ form the forest $\Tree{s}$ under inclusion, which we also call the tree of size $s$ although it may have several roots.
%
The nodes contained in no other node are its roots, and the children of a node are the largest nodes strictly inside it.
%
The \textit{own node} of a vertex $v$ with $\core{s}{v}\ge1$, denoted as $\own{s}{v}$, is the $\Nuc{s}{\core{s}{v}}$ containing $v$.
\end{definition}
Nesting and maximality give three facts about tops~\cite{firstNucleus}.
%
The top of a node is the smallest core value of its vertices: every vertex has at least the top, and if all had more, the node would have the two properties one level higher and, being maximal, would be a core there.
%
In particular the top of $\own{s}{v}$ is $\core{s}{v}$.
%
Tops increase strictly from a root down to its descendants, since a child with a top of at most its parent's top could be extended to the parent without losing a property.
%
The nodes containing $v$ form a path from a root down to $\own{s}{v}$, and for $1\le k\le\core{s}{v}$ the community of $v$ at $(s,k)$ is the node nearest the root with top at least $k$ on this path, since a larger node with top at least $k$ would strictly contain it.
%
Later sections refer to the community by this description.

\begin{example}\label{ex:tree}
Consider Figure~\ref{fig:example}; Figure~\ref{fig:strees} draws the trees of its four sizes, $2$ to $5$.
%
At size $2$ the root holds all $15$ vertices with top $3$ (every vertex has at least three neighbours, $b_1$ exactly three) and has two children of top $4$, $A$ and $\{x,u_1,\dots,w_3\}$, the two $4$-cores of Example~\ref{ex:nucleus}.
%
At size $3$ the forest has two roots and no other node, $N=B\cup\{u_1,\dots,w_3\}$ with top $3$ and $A$ with top $6$: no triangle joins them, and $A$ is the $(3,k)$-core for every $k$ from $1$ to $6$.
%
At size $4$ the roots are $A$ (top $4$) and $B$ (top $1$), and at size $5$ the forest is $A$ alone.
%
The own node of $b_1$ is the root at size $2$, $N$ at size $3$ and $B$ at size $4$.
%
The community of $x$ at $(2,3)$ is the root, the node nearest the root with top at least $3$ on the path from $\own{2}{x}=\{x,u_1,\dots,w_3\}$.
\end{example}

\stitle{The index of one size.}
The tree of one size gives an index for that size~\cite{fastHierarchy,Fang2016CLtree}.
%
The vertices with $\core{s}{v}\ge1$ are written into one array by a depth-first traversal of $\Tree{s}$, each vertex when the traversal reaches its own node, so the vertices of every node form one contiguous slice of the array.
%
In Figure~\ref{fig:strees} every node is drawn as a bar over its slice, and the vertices under a bar and under none of its child bars are those whose own node it is.
%
Every node stores its top, its parent and the two ends of its slice, and every vertex stores its own node and its core value.
%
A community query climbs from the own node to the node nearest the root whose top is at least $k$ and copies that node's slice, and a value query reads the stored value.

\stitle{One hierarchy per size.}
An index that serves every size stores, for each size, one tree and one array, together with every core value.
%
We call this index \strees, and it is the baseline of Section~\ref{sec:exp}.
%
A vertex $v$ has a nonzero value at every size from $2$ to $\omg{v}$ (Section~\ref{sec:prelim}), so it appears in $\omg{v}-1$ arrays, one per size.
%
The arrays therefore hold $\sum_v(\omg{v}-1)$ entries, $44$ for the $15$ vertices of Figure~\ref{fig:example}, as Figure~\ref{fig:strees} shows.
%
There are as many own-node pointers, and as many stored values.
%
Building \strees means one decomposition per size, $499$ of them on \webuk (Section~\ref{sec:intro}).
%
