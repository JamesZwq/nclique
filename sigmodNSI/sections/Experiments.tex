\section{Experimental Evaluation}
\label{sec:exp}

\stitle{Hardware.}
All algorithms are implemented in C++ and compiled with \texttt{-O3}\footnote{Code and data at \url{https://github.com/anonymous/nsi}.}.
%
Experiments were conducted on a server with $96$ cores and $503$GB of memory, running Linux; every run is single-threaded and executed alone to avoid interference.

\stitle{Algorithms.}
We compare three methods.
%
\specnd{} is our one-build sweep (Algorithm~\ref{alg:specnd}).
%
\specndbase{} shares the build but peels every cell in full, disabling the chain certificate; it isolates the certificate from the shared build.
%
\cnd~\cite{LocalNuclear} is the state-of-the-art per-cell algorithm, run once per cell of the range; it uses all $96$ cores through its parallel counting, while \specnd{} uses one.

\stitle{Datasets.}
We use twelve real graphs from four domains, listed in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}.
%
Web graphs (\daWIT, \daWUK) and structural and fluid-dynamics matrices (\daPKa, \daPWT, \daLDR, \daRAE, \daPKb, \daNAS) have large, tightly overlapping cliques; collaboration graphs (\daDBL, \daAST, \daHEP) are clique-rich but sparser; social graphs (\daEPN, \daYTB) have many small, loosely overlapping cliques.
%
The largest, \daLDR, has over $20$ million edges.

\stitle{Metrics.}
We report end-to-end time and peak memory for computing a whole spectrum, a range of four cells at a fixed order $r$.
%
Unless stated otherwise the order is $r{=}4$ and the range is $s{=}5$ to $8$, applied identically to every graph.
%
We set a two-hour budget per cell and a $300$GB memory budget; a method that exceeds either on a cell cannot complete that cell, and we mark such a cell as out of budget.
%
A per-cell spectrum is out of budget when any of its cells is.
%
Correctness follows from the theorems of Sections~\ref{sec:theory}--\ref{sec:algo}; on every cell where a per-cell run completes, the output of \specnd{} is bit-identical to it.

\expsection{Spectrum Time and Memory}
Table~\ref{tab:main} reports the end-to-end time and memory to compute the whole $r{=}4$ spectrum with \specnd{} and \cnd, using the same order and range on every graph.
%
On the eight clique-structured graphs, \specnd{} wins by orders of magnitude.
%
On five of them (\daWIT, \daWUK, \daPKa, \daPWT, \daLDR), \cnd{} cannot complete any cell within the $300$GB budget, while \specnd{} finishes the whole spectrum in $0$ to $77$ seconds.
%
On \daRAE{} and \daPKb, \cnd{} finishes but takes $294$ and $1{,}302$ seconds against \specnd{}'s $0.2$ and $1.7$ seconds, speedups of $1{,}600\times$ and $770\times$, with $140\times$ and $111\times$ less memory.
%
On \daDBL{} and \daNAS, the speedups are $35\times$ and $15\times$.
%
On the collaboration and social graphs \cnd{}'s per-cell counting is nearly flat in $s$, so the spectrum comparison narrows to $1.5\times$ on \daAST{} and near parity on \daYTB.
%
This experiment demonstrates that \specnd{} turns the infeasible into seconds on clique-structured graphs and remains competitive elsewhere.

\expsection{Scalability with the Order}
Figure~\ref{fig:scal_r} shows how the whole-spectrum time grows with the order $r$ on \daWIT{} and \daDBL, for the range $s{=}r{+}1$ to $r{+}4$.
%
The x-axis is the order $r$ and the y-axis is time in seconds on a log scale.
%
\specnd{} scales gracefully: on \daWIT{} the whole spectrum grows from $0.8$ seconds at $r{=}3$ to $15$ seconds at $r{=}7$, and on \daDBL{} from $2.9$ to $97$ seconds, while memory stays under $3$ and $14$GB.
%
By contrast \cnd{} already needs $56$ minutes and $310$GB for the single cell $(3,4)$ on \daWIT{} and exceeds the budget for every larger order, so \specnd{}'s entire $r{=}7$ spectrum is faster than one \cnd{} cell at $r{=}3$.
%
This experiment demonstrates that the advantage grows with the order, because \cnd{}'s working state grows with the number of $r$-cliques while \specnd{}'s grows with the far smaller number of patterns.

\expsection{Scalability with the Range}
Figure~\ref{fig:scal_s} shows how the whole-spectrum time grows as the range widens, with $r{=}4$ fixed and the top cell $s_{\max}$ increased from $6$ to $12$.
%
The x-axis is $s_{\max}$ and the y-axis is time in seconds.
%
Widening the range is almost free: on \daWIT{} the whole spectrum grows from $1.4$ seconds at six cells to $1.7$ seconds at twelve, and on \daDBL{} from $5.3$ to $6.1$ seconds, while memory stays flat at $0.8$ and $1.1$GB.
%
Each additional cell costs about $0.05$ seconds on \daWIT{} and $0.1$ seconds on \daDBL, independent of how many $s$-cliques the cell contains.
%
This experiment demonstrates the central consequence of the chain certificate: a certified cell costs one integer comparison per pattern, so the width of the spectrum is nearly free.

\expsection{Effect of the Chain Certificate}
The certificate, not the shared build alone, is what collapses the cost.
%
\specndbase{} shares the build but peels every cell in full; on \daHEP{} and \daAST{} it exceeds the two-hour per-cell budget, while \specnd{} finishes the same spectra in $167$ and $66$ seconds, a difference of more than $40\times$.
%
The same effect appears at the cell level against per-cell recomputation with our own engine: on \daDBL{} at $r{=}4$ the cell $s{=}8$ costs a full peel $655$ seconds and \specnd{} $0.07$ seconds, a reduction of $9{,}364\times$.
%
This experiment demonstrates that certification is the source of the speedup: once a pattern certifies, its remaining spectrum is arithmetic.

\expsection{Index Size and Query Time}
Table~\ref{tab:index} reports the size of \nsi{} and its query latency on the eight clique-structured graphs.
%
\nsi{} stores a whole spectrum at $0.001$ to $2.2$ bytes per $r$-clique: on \daWUK{} the four-cell spectrum of $838$ million $r$-cliques fits in $1$MB, and on \daLDR{} the spectrum of over one billion $r$-cliques fits in $54$MB.
%
Point queries take $191$ to $794$ nanoseconds and full-spectrum queries $388$ to $985$ nanoseconds, on a workload of $100$ thousand randomly sampled $r$-cliques per graph.
%
The index is built as a byproduct of the sweep at no measurable extra cost.
%
This experiment demonstrates that the whole spectrum is not only computed but stored and queried, in megabytes and nanoseconds.

\expsection{Certification Coverage}
The wins track one quantity: the fraction of $r$-cliques certified per cell.
%
On the web and matrix graphs every cell above the boundary certifies $100\%$ of $r$-cliques, and on \daWUK{} every region is mergeable, so the whole spectrum is closed form with no peeling at all.
%
On \daDBL{} certification is $100\%$ and on \daAST{} it is $99.96\%$, leaving a residue of a few thousand patterns out of millions.
%
On the social graphs certification is lower and rises with the order: on \daYTB{} it grows from $79\%$ at $r{=}3$ to $99.98\%$ at $r{=}6$.
%
This experiment explains the earlier results: certification coverage is the single variable that separates the order-of-magnitude wins from the modest ones.

\expsection{The Diagonal Transfer}
Theorem~\ref{thm:diagonal} bounds the core value at cell $(r{+}1,r{+}2)$ by the minimum over $r$-subcliques at cell $(r,r{+}1)$ minus one.
%
We tested it on all $4$-cliques of three graphs by comparing the exact cells $(3,4)$ and $(4,5)$: across $156$ million $4$-cliques the bound holds with zero violations, and it is an equality for $100\%$ of the $4$-cliques on \daGRQ{} and \daHEP{} and $23.4\%$ on \daEPN.
%
The equality on collaboration graphs shows that the diagonal recurrence holds there for every single $4$-clique.
